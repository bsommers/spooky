# Scorer calibration (Phase 7)

The local AI-likeness scorer (`src/scoring/scorer.ts`) is a weighted mean of six interpretable text features. This page
records what was measured on a labeled fixture corpus and what that does and does not support.

Every number lives in the generated block below, which `npm run calibrate -- --update-doc` rewrites from the code and
the corpus. `tests/system/calibration.test.ts` regenerates it and fails on any difference, so a changed weight, phrase,
feature or fixture cannot leave a stale table behind. Prose outside the block states no measured number.

## How to read this

- The score ranks a random AI sample above a random human sample with the probability the headline AUC gives, on this
  corpus, with the margin of error the interval gives. It is a weak signal. Scores cluster in the middle of the range, so
  there is no meaningful absolute threshold; do not treat 0.5 as "this is AI".
- **The ship gate is a point estimate.** The test enforces that the AUC reaches the gate and that the interval's lower
  bound is above chance. It does NOT enforce that the lower bound reaches the gate, and the table says whether it does.
  "AUC >= 0.70" is therefore not a statistically established claim about this scorer.
- **Genre confound.** The headline AUC mixes two comparisons: modern assistant prose against 19th-century literary prose,
  and against modern technical writing (RFCs). The table breaks them out; the first is much easier than the second.
- **Typography confound.** The AI fixtures contain no em dashes, semicolons, curly quotes or other non-ASCII
  characters, while about half of the human fixtures do. The `punctuation` feature rewards exactly those marks (it counts
  the em dash as a human marker), so on this corpus it partly measures how the fixtures were typed, not how they were
  written. Real assistant output is often em-dash-heavy; on such text this feature can point the wrong way. The
  neutralized rows in the table normalize those marks in both classes. They remove only the marks named there: the
  feature also counts colons, quotes, parentheses and so on, and those still differ between the classes.
- **Features that point the wrong way.** `lexicalDiversity` and `repetition` assume machine text has a narrower
  vocabulary and repeats phrases more. On this corpus the opposite holds (the RFCs and the 19th-century prose repeat
  function-word phrases; the short AI samples do not). They lower the overall AUC at the shipped weights, and the test
  checks that this sentence is still true. The defaults were not changed in response, because choosing weights after
  seeing 73 samples would fit those 73 samples.
- **`cliche` is contaminated** (see "What was and was not tuned"). Do not read its AUC, or the headline AUC that includes
  it, as an out-of-sample figure. The table gives the headline with `cliche` at weight 0 next to the shipped one.
- `paragraphUniformity` needs three paragraphs, so it is left out for the human excerpts that have fewer (their weights
  renormalize). Availability itself differs between the classes.
- The corpus has no modern casual human writing (blogs, emails, forum posts, student essays), which is where such a
  scorer would actually be used, and no AI text from any model other than one, or from prompts that ask for a human
  voice.
- The next fix is a larger, genre-matched corpus with several generator models and fixtures written without knowledge
  of the feature set, then re-deciding the weights and the phrase list on a held-out split.

## Corpus and provenance

Everything is under `tests/fixtures/corpus/`; `manifest.json` has one entry per file (source, URL or generating prompt,
license, note) and the harness refuses a file without an entry or an entry without a file.

- **Human.** Excerpts of whole paragraphs, line wraps joined, no other edits (excerpt offsets are not recorded, so that
  claim cannot be audited from the manifest). The literary samples are public-domain prose from Project Gutenberg
  published 1787 to 1907 (essays, novels, memoir, natural history). The RFC samples are sections of IETF RFCs from 1995
  to 2020 (informational and process documents). All of it predates widespread LLM text. **Licensing:** RFCs published
  before November 2008 may permit verbatim reproduction only, and these excerpts have their line wraps joined, so the
  manifest does not call them freely reproducible; see `NOTICE` for what is bundled and under which terms. Swapping the
  pre-2008 RFCs for later ones would change the corpus and every number above, so it was not done here.
- **AI.** Written by Claude (Sonnet 5.5) in the session that built this phase, one text per prompt, no style steering
  beyond the genre named in the prompt (essay, explainer, email, blog post, product copy, fiction scene, README, speech,
  review, newsletter, plain Q&A answer). The prompt is stored per file.

## What was and was not tuned

- The feature curves and the six default weights were committed before the corpus was added and have not changed
  since. That ordering is self-attested by the author (git records the commit order, not when each fixture text was
  written).
- The stock-phrase list (`config/scorer.ts`) was **not** fixed independently of the corpus. It was written by the same
  model that wrote the AI fixtures, after them, with them in context, and phrases that looked like echoes of a specific
  fixture were removed. Removing phrases because of what the fixtures contain is tuning on the corpus. The `cliche`
  feature is therefore test-set contaminated by construction, and its AUC is optimistic by an unknown amount. The
  fixtures, the phrase list and this document were all authored by one model in one session.
- No weight, curve or threshold was adjusted to reach the gate. The gate is a point-estimate gate: see the table for
  how it stands against the interval.

## What this score is not

- Not a detector verdict. It measures stylistic regularity that assistant-written text often shows and that careful
  human writing sometimes shows too. A low score does not show a text is human; a high one does not show it is AI.
- Not a calibrated probability.
- Third-party detectors use different signals and change without notice; their scores do not line up with this one.
  The `DetectorAdapter` ensemble averages whatever the adapters return and records any that fail or return an invalid
  value; it does not validate their accuracy. **Direction:** a detector must report 1 = machine-generated and
  0 = human-written, like the local score. The ensemble cannot tell a detector that reports P(human) from one that
  reports P(AI), and averaging it in would silently invert that member; front such a service with a shim that inverts it.
- External adapters send the (already redacted) text to a third-party endpoint. They run only when you name them
  (`--detector <name>` / `engine.score(text, { detectors: [...] })`), and only for detectors declared in
  `scorer.detectors`. A detector URL must be https (plain http only for localhost, 127.0.0.0/8 and ::1, with or
  without a key), and its `apiKeyEnv` must be an upper-case name ending in `_KEY`, `_API_KEY` or `_TOKEN` that is not
  another system's credential (`AWS_*`, `GITHUB_*`, `*_SECRET_*`, ...), so a config file you did not write cannot
  point a variable of its choosing at a host of its choosing.
