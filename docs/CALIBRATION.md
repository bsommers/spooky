# Scorer calibration (Phase 7, corpus v2)

The local AI-likeness scorer (`src/scoring/scorer.ts`) is a weighted mean of six interpretable text features. This page
records what was measured on a labeled corpus, under a dev / test split, and what that does and does not support.

Every number lives in the generated block below, which `npm run calibrate -- --update-doc` rewrites from the code and
the corpus. `tests/system/calibration.test.ts` regenerates it and fails on any difference, so a changed weight, phrase,
feature or fixture cannot leave a stale table behind. Prose outside the block states no measured number.

## Procedure (what was decided on which data)

1. The corpus was enlarged (see "Corpus and provenance") and each file assigned to **dev** or **test** by
   `sha256("dag-calibration-v2\n" + text) mod 100 < 50`. The seed was fixed before any v2 file was scored; a file's side
   cannot be changed by renaming it.
2. All tuning used `npm run calibrate -- --dev-only`, which computes nothing from the test split. Two things were
   decided on dev only: the feature weights (feature AUC minus 0.5, normalized, with the eligibility rule in the block)
   and which candidate stock words are too common in human prose (the rule in the block, human dev texts only).
3. The weights were adopted because a five-fold cross-validated dev AUC beat the previous defaults by a margin fixed in
   advance (the table says by how much). The eligibility rule (a feature must be computable on at least half of the
   texts of both classes) was added after looking at the dev per-feature table, because `paragraphUniformity` was
   computable on only a handful of human dev texts; that is a decision made on dev data, and it is disclosed here.
4. The test split was then scored once with the shipped defaults. Nothing (weights, phrases, curve constants, corpus
   files, split) was changed in response to the test numbers. Shortcomings found along the way (one, the length
   mismatch, was already visible in the dev word counts) are listed under "Known weaknesses" instead of being fixed
   after the fact, because changing the corpus after the test run would be fitting to it.

### Stock-phrase list: rule and result

Candidates are the 29 single style words named in the text or figure labels of Kobak et al. 2024 (arXiv:2406.07016), a study of excess vocabulary in published abstracts; no word comes from the AI fixtures. ## How to read this

- The headline is the **test split**, scored once. The score ranks a random AI sample above a random human sample with
  the probability the test AUC gives, on this corpus, with the margin of error the interval gives. It is a weak signal.
  Scores cluster in the middle of the range, so there is no meaningful absolute threshold; do not treat 0.5 as "this is
  AI".
- **The old ship gate (point estimate at least 0.70) is reported, not enforced.** The v1 corpus met it, but that corpus
  compared modern assistant prose with 19th-century literature and RFCs. On the genre-matched corpus the held-out
  figure is what the block says; the block states plainly whether the gate is met. The test enforces only that the
  interval's lower bound on the test split clears chance, and that this page's gate line matches the measurement. The
  gate was not lowered and nothing was fitted to the test split to reach it.
- **v1 files vs v2 files.** The test-split table separates them. The original v1 comparison is much easier than the
  modern genre-matched one; the v2 line is the more honest estimate of use on contemporary text.
- **Features that point the wrong way.** `lexicalDiversity` and `repetition` assume machine text has a narrower
  vocabulary and repeats phrases more. On both the dev and test split the opposite holds, so the dev-only rule gave them
  weight 0. They remain in the code and in the breakdown; they simply carry no weight by default. The test checks that
  both still point the wrong way on dev and test.
- **`punctuation` carries most of the weight.** It is the best single feature on dev and on test, and it is also the
  feature most exposed to typographic accident (it rewards semicolons, dashes, colons, quotes). The v2 AI samples
  include em dashes, semicolons and curly quotes (counts in the block) so the marks are no longer a pure label leak,
  and the "typography neutralized" rows show what is left when they are flattened in both classes. They remove only the
  marks named; colons, parentheses and the rest still differ between classes.
- **`cliche` is now weak but not contaminated.** Its word list comes from a published study and a human-text frequency
  filter, not from the AI samples. The dev-only filter uses about twelve thousand human words, so it is crude: some
  ordinary words (`significant`, `across`) survived because they occurred too few times in that sample to be dropped.
  The feature adds little on the test split (the `cliche` weight 0 row is the check).
- `paragraphUniformity` needs three paragraphs; most samples of both classes are single paragraphs, so it is rarely
  computable and, by the eligibility rule, carries no default weight.

## Known weaknesses (not fixed, because fixing them after the test run would be fitting to it)

- **Length is not matched.** The AI samples are shorter than the human ones (mean words in the block). Word count alone
  separates the test split better than the scorer does, and the scorer's features (rates per 100 words, window
  statistics) are not length-neutral. The "length-trimmed" rows cut every text to its first whole sentences within a
  fixed word limit; that figure is lower than the headline and is the better estimate of the scorer's own signal. The
  dev weights were fitted on untrimmed text.
- **One generator, one author.** Every AI sample, v1 and v2, was written by the same model (Claude, Sonnet 5.5) in one
  working session, across registers (formal, casual, technical, narrative) but with no other model, no prompt asking
  for a particular voice, and no human editing of AI text. The same model also chose the topics from the list of human
  samples. The scorer's score on other generators is unmeasured.
- **Human labels are provenance, not proof.** The v2 US-government pages were retrieved in November 2026. Pages posted
  after 2022 could in principle have had AI assistance that the page does not disclose; the Project Gutenberg texts
  (published before 1929) and the 2017 White House transcripts cannot. The split into dev and test cuts across both.
- **No casual modern human prose.** Public-domain and US-government text is formal, narrative or technical. Blogs,
  emails, forum posts and student essays are not represented in the human class, which is where such a scorer would be
  used. The casual-register AI samples therefore have no casual human counterpart; the corpus cannot say how the scorer
  does on it.
- **Paired topics across the split.** Each v2 AI sample was written to match the genre and topic of a v2 human sample;
  the split is by hash, so a pair can straddle it. This does not leak labels, but the dev and test sets are not
  topic-independent.
- Per-genre AUCs in the block rest on a handful of samples per class and are shown for orientation only.
- The scorer is a weak, stylistic signal on this corpus; do not rely on it to decide authorship of any text.

## Corpus and provenance

Everything is under `tests/fixtures/corpus/`; `manifest.json` has one entry per file (source, URL or generating prompt,
license, note, set, genre) and the harness refuses a file without an entry or an entry without a file.

- **Human, v1 (39 files).** Public-domain prose from Project Gutenberg published 1787 to 1907 and sections of IETF RFCs
  from 1995 to 2020. See `NOTICE` for the RFC terms.
- **Human, v2 (102 files).** Fetched with `curl`/HTTP in November 2026 and extracted verbatim; each manifest entry has the
  source URL, retrieval date and license. Seventy-two excerpts are from US-government web pages (NASA, NIH, NIST, FDA,
  NSF, NOAA, DOE) and 2017 White House speech transcripts, which are works of the US government and not subject to
  copyright (17 U.S.C. 105; NASA pages may embed third-party material, and no embedded media is included). Thirty are
  from Project Gutenberg books published 1899 to 1925 (public domain in the US). Excerpts are whole consecutive
  paragraphs; for the web pages, short paragraphs, credits, captions and contact lines are skipped by a filter, and the
  start offset is chosen by a hash of the URL. Stage directions such as "(Applause.)" were removed from the speeches.
  No human text was written or altered by the author of this work. The fetch and selection scripts are in
  `scripts/corpus-v2/`.
- **AI, v1 (34 files).** Written by Claude (Sonnet 5.5) in the Phase 7 session, one text per prompt, no style steering
  beyond the genre named in the prompt.
- **AI, v2 (106 files).** Written by the same model for this calibration, to cover the same genres and topics as the v2
  human samples (science news, health science, technical / policy, regulatory, speech, nonfiction essay, narrative
  fiction) in four registers (formal, casual, technical, narrative). About half contain em dashes, semicolons or curly
  quotes as the model naturally produced them; in six files of the first batch, straight quotes were converted to
  curly by script (the manifest note says which). The prompt is stored per file. They were written before any v2 scoring
  was run.

## What was and was not tuned

- Curve constants (`FEATURE_PARAMS`) are unchanged from Phase 7 and were not tuned on v2 data.
- The six default weights and the stock-word list were re-derived on the dev split by the stated rules. The previous
  weights are kept as `LEGACY_WEIGHTS` in `scripts/calibrate.ts` so the comparison can be re-run.
- The earlier stock-phrase list (written by the same model as the AI fixtures, with them in context) is gone. The
  new list does not draw on any AI sample.
- No threshold or gate was changed. Nothing was changed after the test split was scored.

## What this score is not

- Not a detector verdict. It measures stylistic regularity that assistant-written text often shows and that careful
  human writing sometimes shows too. A low score does not show a text is human; a high one does not show it is AI.
- Not a calibrated probability.
- Third-party detectors use different signals and change without notice; their scores do not line up with this one.
  The `DetectorAdapter` ensemble averages whatever the adapters return and records any that fail or return an invalid
  value; it does not validate their accuracy. **Direction:** a detector must report 1 = machine-generated and
  0 = human-written, like the local score. The ensemble cannot tell a detector that reports P(human) from one that
  reports P(AI), and averaging it in would silently invert that member; front such a service with a shim that inverts it.
- External adapters send the (already redacted) text to a third-party endpoint. They run only when you name them
  (`--detector <name>` / `engine.score(text, { detectors: [...] })`), and only for detectors declared in
  `scorer.detectors`. A detector URL must be https (plain http only for localhost, 127.0.0.0/8 and ::1, with or
  without a key), and its `apiKeyEnv` must be an upper-case name ending in `_KEY`, `_API_KEY` or `_TOKEN` that is not
  another system's credential (`AWS_*`, `GITHUB_*`, `*_SECRET_*`, ...), so a config file you did not write cannot
  point a variable of its choosing at a host of its choosing.
